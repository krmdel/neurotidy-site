Short answer: for breaking any task into steps, goblin.tools Magic ToDo is the one most ADHD threads recommend. For a cleaning schedule that tracks itself, Tody or Sweepy, both free with paid extras. For someone to clean alongside, Focusmate gives three free sessions a week. For paper you can print without handing over an email, ADHD Printable and Neurotidy both give direct PDFs. Below is what each one actually does, what costs money, and where each falls short.

How do you pick a free ADHD cleaning tool?

Start from where you get stuck, not from the feature list. ADHD cleaning usually breaks at one of four points, and each tool here is good at one of them.

  • You cannot start. The blocker is initiation. A body double (Focusmate, or a friend on a call) or a timer that hands you one step at a time does more than any list.
  • You do not know what the steps are. The task feels like one giant blob. A breakdown tool turns it into pieces: Magic ToDo for anything, the Neurotidy chore breakdown for common home chores.
  • You forget what is due. The problem is memory, not effort. A schedule app like Tody or Sweepy keeps track so you do not have to.
  • You lose the list. Apps live behind a lock screen full of distractions. Paper taped where the job happens (a fridge, a bathroom mirror) works for a lot of people, which is the case for printables.

Most people end up with one of each kind rather than one perfect tool. A schedule app plus a printed bad-day card is a common pairing in ADHD threads.

goblin.tools Magic ToDo

Magic ToDo takes a task you type and splits it into smaller steps, and you can ask it to split any step again. It is the tool ADHD subreddits name most often when someone says they cannot start, and it covers far more than cleaning: there are sibling tools for estimating time, rewording messages and planning meals. The site says it is kept free by sponsors, there are phone apps, and it now has optional accounts for syncing lists.

Where it falls short: the steps are generated each time, so they vary, and it does not build in a lower-effort version for bad days. You also have to know what to type, which is hard when the whole room is the problem.

Tody

Tody tracks how long it has been since each area was cleaned and shows what is due, instead of making you follow a fixed weekly rota. If you miss a week, the rota does not collapse; overdue areas simply move up the list. The core plan is free on iPhone and Android with no ads. Premium is $9.99 a year and adds a focus timer and multiple homes; household sharing sits in a higher tier.

Where it falls short: setting it up takes a sitting of its own, which is exactly the kind of task that gets put off. It tells you what is due, not how to start it.

Sweepy

Sweepy turns chores into points and lets you split them across a household, which suits people who are motivated by streaks and small rewards. The app is free to start; the paid tier is $3.99 a month or $19.99 a year, and reviews often mention that sharing with other people sits behind it.

Where it falls short: gamification wears off for a lot of ADHD brains once the novelty goes, and there is no lifetime option if you dislike subscriptions.

Focusmate

Focusmate pairs you with a stranger on video for a 25, 50 or 75 minute session. You each say what you are doing, then get on with it. It is built for work, but plenty of people use it for laundry and dishes because having someone present is what gets them moving. The free plan is three sessions a week; unlimited is from $8 a month billed yearly.

Where it falls short: you need a camera and a scheduled slot, which is a lot to ask on a low day. A friend on a voice call does the same job for free. The body doubling guide covers the low-effort versions.

ADHD Printable

A free library of PDF cleaning planners, checklists and routine sheets for adults with ADHD. Every file is a direct download; the site says plainly there is no email, no app and no sign-up. With about a dozen printables, it has the widest choice of planner layouts here.

Where it falls short: they are planners and lists, so they help with remembering and less with starting. You still have to decide where to begin.

Honestly ADHD

An ADHD blog that publishes a free cleaning checklist next to a guide on how to clean with ADHD, and sells planners and spreadsheets in its shop. Worth it if you want the explanation and the printable in one place.

Where it falls short: it runs an email newsletter, so check whether a download asks for your address, and the cleaning material is one part of a much broader ADHD site.

Neurotidy

Neurotidy makes printables and three small browser tools, all built on one rule: the first step is almost nothing, the time is honest, and every task has a bad-day version that still counts. The free part is the one-page cleaning checklist, the weekly cleaning planner, five task cards, the room reset timer, the chore breakdown and the task card maker. All PDFs are direct downloads and the tools need no account and save nothing. The one paid thing is a 20-card deck, $19 once.

Where it falls short: it is a small, new site. There is no app, no reminders, no sync between people and no streaks. The chore breakdown only knows common home chores, about two dozen of them, so for anything outside the home Magic ToDo is the better pick.

What about ChatGPT or another chatbot?

A general chatbot will happily break a chore into steps, and many people with ADHD use one exactly that way, sometimes with a photo of the room. It works. The trade-offs are that the answer changes every time, it tends to give you ten steps when you needed two, and the app you open it in is full of other things to do. If it works for you, keep it. If it does not, the tools above are narrower on purpose.
