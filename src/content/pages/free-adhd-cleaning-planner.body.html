Why a planner, when a checklist already exists

A checklist answers "what do I do in this room". A planner answers the question that actually stops most ADHD adults on a weekday evening: "what do I do today". Deciding is the expensive part. With ADHD the cost of choosing between six rooms is often higher than the cost of cleaning one of them, so the planner removes the choice. Monday is the kitchen. That is the whole decision, made in advance, by the sheet.

The second thing it does is name the first physical step for every day. Not "clean the kitchen" but "fill the sink with hot soapy water and drop everything in". The guide to ADHD cleaning paralysis explains why wanting to start and being able to start are separate systems; the short version is that a named first step is the on-ramp, and the planner prints one for every day so you never have to generate it yourself.

The week, as printed

Every day has three parts: a theme, a first step, and a shaded bad-day line. Set a 10-minute timer, do the first step, keep going until it rings, then stop even mid-task. On a day you cannot function, do only the bad-day line and tick the box. It counts.

  • Monday, kitchen. First step: fill the sink with hot soapy water and drop everything in. Then bin the wrappers, then wipe the one counter you look at most. Bad day: rinse one mug for tomorrow.
  • Tuesday, laundry. First step: carry one basket to the machine and start it before you do anything else. Fold only what comes out today; the old pile waits. Bad day: dirty clothes off the floor into the basket.
  • Wednesday, bathroom. First step: spray the sink and the toilet before your shower, wipe both after, towels to a hook. Bad day: wipe the sink with the towel you just used.
  • Thursday, floors and paths. First step: clear the walking path from the door to the bed, then to the sofa. Shoes to one spot. Sweep only the path. Bad day: clear the path to the bed.
  • Friday, one doom pile. First step: pick the pile you walk past most and sort it into four boxes, bin, belongs elsewhere, keep here, decide later, until the timer rings. "Decide later" is allowed. Bad day: take three things off the top and bin or put them away.
  • Saturday, your choice of room. First step: walk in, say out loud the one thing that bothers you most, fix only that. Cups back to the kitchen on the way out. Bad day: cups from one room back to the kitchen.
  • Sunday, rest and reset. No cleaning. Five minutes in the evening: tomorrow's clothes out, one clear surface, bag by the door. Circle next week's one big thing. Bad day: tomorrow's clothes out, and that is the whole job.

Underneath the week sits the daily bare minimum, the three things that keep a home from getting worse on any day at any energy: rubbish that smells goes out, one clear path through each room, tomorrow's first thing set out. The bare-minimum cleaning day guide is the longer version of that idea.

The three dials at the top

One big thing. One line for the single larger job this week, and only one. This is the useful half of the 1-3-5 rule: a capped list stops the week from becoming a list of everything you feel guilty about.

Energy today. Three boxes: low, mid, high. Low means you do only the bad-day line. Mid is the 10-minute timer. High is 20 minutes, and then you stop anyway, because the all-or-nothing four-hour clean is how the churn and burn pattern starts and it always ends in three weeks of nothing.

Reward after the timer. Write it down before you start. A reward you chose in advance works; a reward you improvise at the end of ten minutes of dishes does not, because by then you have already decided you did not do enough to deserve one.

Who this is for, and who it is not for

It is for anyone whose week falls apart at "what should I clean today". That includes ADHD adults, people waiting on a diagnosis, and anyone with a brain that stalls on choosing. It is not a deep-cleaning schedule and it will not get a house spotless; it keeps a home from sliding and makes the sliding stop feeling like a character flaw. If your home is past the point where a 10-minute reset makes a visible difference, start with the low-energy list for a week first, then come back to the planner.
